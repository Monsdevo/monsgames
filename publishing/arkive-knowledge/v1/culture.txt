Writing, Cities, and Historical Evidence

WRITING AND MEMORY
Writing lets people record information across distance and time. Early written records include inventories, tax lists, laws, religious texts, and correspondence. A surviving record may document the priorities of a government, institution, or individual. The survival of a record also depends on materials, storage, and chance.

CITIES AND TRADE
Cities tend to concentrate people, markets, institutions, and infrastructure. Transport routes connect them to resources and customers. Trade carries goods alongside techniques, languages, and ideas. Exchange can occur through markets, gifts, contracts, or networks of intermediaries.

PRIMARY AND SECONDARY SOURCES
A primary source is direct evidence relevant to a historical question, such as a letter, object, photograph, or contemporary record. A secondary source interprets or synthesizes evidence. An object or document can be primary evidence for one question and secondary evidence for another. To compare accounts, check who made them, when they were made, and what the author could have known.

ORAL HISTORY
Oral history records people's accounts of lived events. It preserves experiences that may be scarce in written archives. Memory, the interview setting, and the questions asked can shape an account. Comparing different interviews and other available records can clarify where details agree and where they differ.
